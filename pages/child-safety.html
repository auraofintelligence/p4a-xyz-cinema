Child safety

Homework help, companionship and persuasive design can arrive in the same chat window. Families deserve understandable controls and services designed around children’s wellbeing.

What the children’s survey found

eSafety’s 2026 study surveyed 1,950 Australian children aged 10–17 between 2 February and 4 March. Of all surveyed children, 78% had used an AI assistant. Among children who had used an AI assistant or companion, 20% reported potentially inappropriate or harmful interactions and 32% had shared personal data or potentially sensitive information. Those last two percentages are of users, not all children.

Different protections, different costs

Safer defaults and independent testingLimit sensitive-data retention and test age-appropriate responses before release. Default protection reaches people who never find settings, but tests must cover real use and changing models.
Limits on manipulative designExamine emotional pressure, exclusive relationships and engagement incentives. Clear boundaries can protect children while leaving room for useful, creative and accessible assistance.
Age assurance with minimal dataAge checks can support proportionate access rules but can also collect identity data or exclude users. Compare privacy-preserving methods, error rates, appeals and deletion before selecting one.
Reporting and human helpA visible report button and timely human response make failures actionable. They need funded moderation and escalation, alongside prevention rather than relying on children to police a product.

Adults can act without exposing children

Parents, carers, educators and youth organisations can review a service’s retention settings, complaint route and safeguards with young people. Report specific harms through the service and eSafety’s official routes. For public policy submissions, use aggregated observations and remove names, screenshots and identifying stories. This page does not collect children’s accounts.

A local alternative needs safeguards too

Luke’s local-first Sensorium and Ready S.E.T. learning model offer a way for libraries and community hosts to choose bounded educational tools, hold records locally and retain human supervision. Any pilot still needs child-safe staff practices, access controls, independent testing and clear limits on what it stores. A community label is not a safety certificate.
