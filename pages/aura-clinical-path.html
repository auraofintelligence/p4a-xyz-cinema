From a care task to accountable use

Start with a specific task, the people using the tool and the decision it would support. A memoir, a prescribing check and a claims summary need different evidence. Chamber operation and a digital health application also require their own assessments.

One proposed use is to turn the person's chosen life story, functional patterns and care needs into clinician-readable reports. Relevant information would map to accepted assessment domains and changes over time, with each use and interpretation reviewed.

  1. Define the task and consent

    Specify the information collected, who can see it, the output and its limits. Provide a way to correct records, withdraw participation and continue through a human care route.

  2. Test feasibility and safety

    Begin with small, supervised studies of comprehension, usability, consent and safety. Check whether the task creates confusion, extra work or unwanted disclosure.

  3. Evaluate the intended use

    Clinical partners and participants agree on outcomes before a pilot. Record usefulness, burdens and adverse experiences, including why people stop using the tool. The care rooms set out their application-specific questions.

  4. Meet technical and regulatory requirements

    Assess privacy, cybersecurity, human factors and software-device obligations for the proposed claims. Keep an inspectable record of evidence, changes and unresolved risks.

  5. Keep a professional accountable

    Name the person who reviews outputs, responds to errors and follows up. Record important decisions and overrides. Independent review, training, support and funded clinician time are part of delivery.

A GP example: AI as a duty to check

The GP proposal asks whether a future standard of care should expect suitable, validated AI checks when they are beneficial for a particular task. An unjustified failure to use a useful check could be reviewable. This is a proposed professional standard, not a statement of current negligence law.

Its term ethical overcompliance means doing more than the minimum required check. In practice, the assistant could:

  • Check medicines, allergies, interactions and missing information, and prepare prescribing material for the clinician to verify and authorise.
  • Search source-linked explanations for unusual or unresolved symptoms, showing uncertainty and evidence quality.
  • Draft notes and correspondence, flag follow-up duties and record the clinician's reasons for overriding a check.

Clinical bodies, practices, health services and regulators would need to develop the standard and evaluate the tools. Funding would cover clinician time, integration, security and training. The funding proposal also values time with patients, continuity and prevention. Evaluation would examine missed issues, completed checks, follow-up, patient outcomes, time returned to care and audit findings.
