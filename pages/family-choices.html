Twinkle 29 / Brainstorming scenario

Having children: make the wanted choice possible

Support people to have the family they want, including fewer children or none. Affordability is one factor; health, relationships, care, time, confidence and personal preference matter too.

The question is what prevents people having the family they want. That includes relationships, health, care, time and confidence as well as money; optional shared households are one possibility among several.

Explore the connected work

  • Global Group Marriages - A voluntary adult household and relationship thought experiment exploring care, consent, governance and exit. Source repository
  • Mutual Futures - A connected proposal for productive ownership, paid livelihoods, learning, care and freedom over time. Source repository

How it could work

  1. Services identify unmet needs without setting a birth target or assuming everyone wants children.
  2. Join reproductive and relationship support, secure housing, usable leave and care access around the person's priorities.
  3. Offer optional shared-care and household arrangements with clear individual rights, while testing whether barriers to wanted choices actually fall.

Everyday example

A couple wanting another child can address work hours and caring load; someone choosing no children is not treated as a policy failure.

Who could act and how it could be funded

Health and care services span national and state programmes, with employers and households participating by choice. Cost services separately and assess access and wellbeing rather than paying for a birth count.

Fair-go safeguards

No coercion, preferred family template or fertility promise. Optional shared households need clear entry, consent, property and exit arrangements; nobody owes a public explanation of their decision.

What would show progress

Unmet wanted-family needs; care access; time pressure; informed choice; wellbeing across different household forms.

What we can check

UNFPA’s 2025 survey across 14 countries identifies financial constraints, housing, job insecurity, relationships and fears about the future among barriers to desired family size. Those international results are not an Australian prevalence estimate. ABS birth and fertility statistics measure births, not the number of children people wished to have; intentions need their own evidence.

Choices and tradeoffs

Housing, childcare and secure workReduce recurring costs and uncertainty, with enough homes, care places and staff to make eligibility useful. Subsidies alone do not guarantee a place or affordable rent.
Time, healthcare and shared careUsable leave, reproductive healthcare and flexible work can make a desired family more feasible. Coverage, career penalties and unequal caring burdens need explicit attention.
Wider family and household choicesShared homes and larger care networks may spread time and risk. Consent, privacy, children’s interests and survivable exits matter; no household form suits everyone.

A practical public voice

Ask representatives to evaluate unmet intentions and wellbeing alongside birth counts. Bring examples of the circumstances shaping the family people want: relationships, health, time, care, money, housing, support, personal wishes and confidence in the future. Where useful, compare costs and available services without assuming one family speaks for everyone. Men’s fears about separation, women’s concerns about unequal care and the experiences of single and LGBTQIA+ people all deserve evidence and a fair hearing.

Share care without surrendering a voice

Global Group Marriages explores an optional household structure for sharing care, resources and responsibility while preserving individual autonomy. Compare actual time, costs, entry and exit arrangements with other household choices. A different family structure does not remove every financial pressure or conflict.
