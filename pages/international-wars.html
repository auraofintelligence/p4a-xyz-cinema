Overseas wars: state the purpose before the commitment

The AUKUS, Fiji-Australia and Ocean of Peace submissions explore security through useful shared capability and enduring relationships. Local computing, food, learning, science, culture and disaster resilience could give communities reasons to cooperate before conflict becomes the only way to coordinate. Military commitments would still have to explain purpose, alternatives, consequences and exit.

Peace infrastructure means useful relationships and shared capability that make cooperation worthwhile. The Fiji and Ocean of Peace proposals connect everyday food, skills, culture and resilience to that larger possibility.

How it could work

  1. Publish a non-sensitive commitment brief and parliamentary scrutiny plan before a major decision where practicable.
  2. Compare diplomacy, mediation, aid and civilian capability with the proposed use of force on their actual functions.
  3. Review the commitment against its objectives and harm, and publish reasons to continue, change or end it.

Everyday example

A voter can see what the commitment is meant to achieve and what would cause a change of course, rather than another open-ended assurance.

Who could act and how it could be funded

Defence and foreign affairs are Commonwealth responsibilities. Victoria can support relevant research, education, civil resilience and community services. Proposed alternatives need separate cost and capability cases, not an assumed transfer of defence money.

Fair-go safeguards

Do not equate a civilian facility with a weapon system or promise diplomacy always succeeds. Preserve lawful sensitive detail while keeping public objectives and accountability readable.

What would show progress

Objectives met; civilian consequences; alternatives examined; review decisions; dependencies and lifecycle costs.

What we can check

The UN Charter sets purposes and rules for international peace and security. International humanitarian law governs conduct during armed conflict, including protection of civilians; that is a different legal question from whether resort to force is lawful. Claims about a specific incident need dated, attributable evidence and competent investigation.

Choices and tradeoffs

Diplomacy and conflict preventionNegotiation, mediation and practical cooperation can reduce danger before violence escalates. Leverage, trust, enforcement and the parties’ willingness limit what a process can achieve.
Defence and deterrenceCapability can protect against coercion and aggression. Costs, escalation risk, civilian harm and dependence on partners must be assessed rather than assume every deployment creates security.
Humanitarian protection and accountabilityAid, refugee protection and lawful investigation address human consequences. Access, diversion, impartiality and safe delivery remain difficult in conflict.

A practical public voice

Ask federal representatives for the objective, legal basis, civilian-protection measures, alternatives, costs and exit conditions behind a proposed commitment. Compare independently sourced accounts and distinguish allegation, verified event and judgement. Do not turn uncertainty into invented certainty about who will win or what an intervention guarantees.

Compare peaceful capability

The UNGA81 capability argument and AUKUS submission examine civilian capability and strategic alternatives. Science, health, learning and disaster resilience can be compared with defence choices on their actual purposes, costs and dependencies. A civilian facility and a submarine do different jobs.
