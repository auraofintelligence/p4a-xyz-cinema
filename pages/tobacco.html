Public choices / October 2026

Tobacco

A serious tobacco policy has to help people quit, reduce harm and confront illicit supply without treating smokers as the enemy.

Open the roomTwinkle seeds

Keep three questions separate

Legal tobacco prices, access to quitting support and illicit supply interact, but they are not the same measure. The Senate inquiry into Australia’s illegal tobacco market provides a public evidence trail to examine. A seizure total is not the size of the whole market, and cheap illicit products do not remove tobacco’s health harms.

Senate tobacco inquiryHealth guidance on smoking, vaping and tobacco

Which mix would work?

Tax with affordable cessation supportPrice signals can support quitting, while practical treatment and culturally appropriate help make change more achievable. Cost pressure can fall hardest on people who remain dependent.
Target illicit supply and organised crimeIntelligence-led enforcement and accountable licensing can address illegal distribution. Enforcement costs money and should be judged by durable results, not only raids.
Review the policy mix against outcomesPublish comparable smoking, quitting, affordability and illicit-market indicators. Adjustments need credible evaluation; a simple tax cut or increase is not a complete health or enforcement policy.

Help and participation have separate doors

For help to stop smoking, use Quitline on 13 7848 or talk with a health professional. For policy participation, adults can ask representatives how quitting services, enforcement funding and tax settings are evaluated together. Do not confront suspected sellers or post allegations about neighbours.

Luke’s connection: make the receipt visible

The Civic Ledger proposal can track spending on cessation and enforcement alongside outcomes; the wider mental-health room asks whether support is affordable and continuous. Publish the programme’s purpose, costs, evidence and review date rather than a judgement about the people using it.
