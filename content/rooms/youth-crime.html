Youth crime: respect, opportunity and a reason to participate

Offer young people real belonging, useful opportunities and a say in what they build. Prevention starts with respect and agency, alongside practical support for victims and a lawful response to immediate harm.

Participation starts with being wanted and respected. Paid tasters, creative work, making, learning and local responsibilities could give young people choices worth taking; contribution recognition would remain voluntary.

How it could work

  1. Young people co-design local spaces and activities with trusted paid youth workers, including boys currently missing a useful pathway.
  2. Provide voluntary paid tasters, apprenticeships, creative work, sport, repair and community projects, with transport and access support.
  3. Give participants responsibility, mentoring and routes into continuing work or learning; fund victim support and evaluate serious harm separately.

Everyday example

A teenager helps choose a workshop project, earns a fair wage, learns from someone who respects them and has a genuine next opportunity.

Who could act and how it could be funded

States lead youth services, justice and training partnerships; councils provide local access and employers offer paid pathways. Fund skilled staff, wages, equipment, transport and ongoing places, not just a launch event.

Fair-go safeguards

No forced participation, humiliating labels, compulsory public profiles or basic support tied to compliance. Queens remains women-only and is not offered as the answer for boys. Children and victims retain safety and rights.

What would show progress

Voluntary retention; belonging and respect; sustained work or learning; repeat serious harm; victim access to support.

What we can check

ABS Recorded Crime - Offenders counts alleged offenders proceeded against by police, not convictions or every offence committed. AIHW youth-justice data describe supervision and detention, another population again. Read local offence types, rates, ages and repeat patterns alongside national trends; neither a viral incident nor one national total describes every community.

ABS: alleged-offender measuresAIHW: youth justice 2024-25

Choices and tradeoffs

Victim support and focused enforcementFast support, investigation and proportionate responses can address immediate harm. A larger arrest total alone does not demonstrate fewer future victims.
Early intervention and stable pathwaysSchool engagement, family support, disability assessment, housing and meaningful work can address underlying needs. Programmes require skilled staff, sustained funding and measured outcomes.
Bail support, diversion and rehabilitationSafe accommodation and supervised alternatives can interrupt repeat offending where appropriate. Serious risk still needs a lawful response; evaluate reoffending and victim outcomes rather than slogans about toughness.

A practical public voice

Ask state representatives for local serious-offence, repeat-offending and victim-support measures with comparable dates. Ask funded programmes to report completion, reoffending and wellbeing outcomes. Use official victim-support and police routes for individual incidents; do not publish identifying allegations about children.

Status and sources
