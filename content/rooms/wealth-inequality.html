The economy grew. Whose balance sheet did?

Income pays this week’s bills. Wealth changes the options available next decade. A fair-go debate needs to see both, including debt and access to productive assets.

What we can check

ABS household income and wealth statistics distinguish flows of income from net worth: assets less liabilities. The detailed 2019-20 distribution is a dated survey, not a 2026 snapshot. Aggregate household wealth can rise while some households remain exposed; averages do not reveal the distribution or who can turn an asset into spendable income.

ABS: income, wealth and survey period

Choices and tradeoffs

Tax and transfer reformCan redistribute resources and fund common services. Design must consider avoidance, incentives, transition, asset-rich cash-poor households and who ultimately bears a tax.
Wider productive ownershipEmployee, cooperative or community stakes can share future returns and voice. They also share risk and require finance, governance and protection against concentrated losses.
Universal services and lower essential costsHousing, health, education and transport can expand real opportunity without everyone owning the same assets. Quality, access and sustainable funding determine the benefit.

A practical public voice

Ask for modelling by wealth group, age, tenure and household type, with debt and regional costs visible. Compare gains in disposable income, service access and productive ownership. A one-year average is not evidence that every generation or household is doing better.

Test a change in productive ownership

Mutual Futures explores business succession into shared ownership, technology upgrades and learning. Its capital and succession workbenches make financing and control assumptions inspectable. A case should show debt, wages, reserves, decision rights and who carries losses; shared ownership does not guarantee returns.
